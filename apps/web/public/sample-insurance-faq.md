# Sample Insurance FAQ — FICTIONAL DEMO ONLY

This document was authored for local software testing, released under CC0. It is not an insurer's policy, an insurance recommendation, or a statement of real-world coverage. All product terms and examples below are fictional. Actual insurance terms must be checked in the user's real policy documents. No real premiums, product comparisons, or claim approvals are provided.

## Premium
In this fictional demo, a premium is the amount paid to keep insurance active. The sample policy allows monthly or yearly payments. This FAQ does not state an amount, due date, discount or payment account. The assistant must not invent a premium price or collect payment. For an actual policy, check its premium schedule.
An illustrative distinction: the premium is what a member pays for the policy, while the sum insured describes its coverage ceiling. Paying the premium does not make every treatment eligible.

## Waiting period
The fictional sample health policy has a waiting period of 30 days for illness-related claims after the cover start date. Accident-related claims have no initial waiting period in this sample. Other waiting periods, including pre-existing conditions, are not specified. These are demo terms only and do not describe any real insurer's policy.

## Claim documents
For a claim in this fictional demo, the checklist includes a completed claim form, the policy number, hospital bills, a discharge summary and a photo identity document. This sample does not promise approval or specify a payout date. Do not submit identity documents, policy numbers or medical records to this demo assistant. Ask a real insurer for its official secure claims process.

## Cashless and reimbursement
In this fictional sample, cashless means a participating hospital requests payment directly from the insurer, subject to authorization. Reimbursement means the patient pays first and submits eligible bills for review. Neither process guarantees that a claim is covered. The sample has no hospital network list or authorization deadline, so the assistant cannot identify a covered hospital.
An illustrative example: a hospital can request cashless authorization before treatment; a reimbursement request follows a payment already made by the patient. Eligibility checks still apply in either route.

## Policy cancellation
The sample FAQ contains no cancellation period, refund formula or cancellation fee. The assistant should say that this information is not in the document, and suggest checking the actual policy or contacting the insurer. Do not infer a real statutory cancellation rule from this fictional document.

## Exclusions and coverage
This fictional FAQ does not define exclusions, a coverage limit, family eligibility, maternity cover or pre-existing disease coverage. An assistant must not infer these from the waiting-period example. It should explain that the document does not contain the answer. For actual coverage decisions, consult the real policy and the insurer.

## Sum insured
In this fictional teaching example, sum insured means the policy's stated ceiling for eligible claims, subject to its other terms. It is different from the premium paid to keep the policy active. An illustrative example: having a coverage ceiling does not mean an excluded treatment becomes payable, or that the entire hospital bill will be approved. This FAQ specifies no coverage amount, restoration benefit or guaranteed payout. An actual member should check the coverage limit and conditions in the policy schedule.

## Deductible
In this fictional teaching example, a deductible is an eligible expense amount the member must bear before the policy pays according to its terms. It differs from the premium: a premium keeps cover active, while a deductible concerns sharing an eligible claim expense. An illustrative example: if a claim is below the applicable deductible, the policy may have nothing payable for that claim. This FAQ gives no deductible amount and does not say whether a deductible applies per claim or across the policy year. Those details require the actual policy.

## Co-payment
In this fictional teaching example, co-payment means the member bears a stated share of an eligible claim expense, while the remaining eligible portion is considered under the policy terms. It does not mean sharing every part of a hospital bill, because some expenses may be excluded. An illustrative example: even after a claim is accepted, the member can still owe a co-payment and non-payable expenses. This FAQ gives no percentage or applicability rule. Check the actual co-payment clause rather than assuming the sample describes a real plan.

## Deductible versus co-payment
In this fictional comparison, a deductible is an expense amount borne before the policy pays under its terms; a co-payment is the member's stated share of an eligible expense. Both can affect the member's own spending, but they describe different mechanisms. A premium is a separate payment to keep insurance active. An illustrative example: asking only whether a policy has cashless service does not answer whether a deductible or co-payment leaves expenses for the member. This FAQ does not specify their amounts, percentages, order of application or combined calculation.

## Worked cost-sharing example
Fictional arithmetic example only: deductible Rs.5000 on eligible bill Rs.12000 leaves Rs.7000 for policy review; co-payment 20% on that bill leaves Rs.2400 for the member and Rs.9600 for policy review. These are separate imagined deductible and co-payment cases, not a combined calculation or the sample policy's actual terms. The example assumes an otherwise eligible expense solely to teach the difference. It specifies no real premium, deductible or co-payment, and does not guarantee payment. Use only these stated figures when explaining this worked example.

## Individual and family floater
In this fictional teaching example, an individual policy has a coverage limit assigned to an insured person, while a family floater has a shared limit for the family members listed in its terms. An illustrative example: an eligible claim by one member can use part of the shared floater limit available to the listed family. This FAQ gives no actual family eligibility, coverage amounts, age rules or restoration provision. Neither option is recommended as better for a particular person. Compare the actual schedule and member eligibility before deciding.

## Claim process
For this fictional demo, the claim journey is: confirm the applicable process with the insurer, gather the required claim documents, submit them through the insurer's official secure channel, and track any requests for clarification. Cashless requests are coordinated with a participating hospital; reimbursement requests concern eligible expenses already paid by the patient. The sample checklist includes a claim form, policy number, hospital bills, discharge summary and photo identity document. Submission does not promise acceptance, payment or a deadline. Do not send these sensitive documents to this demo assistant.

## Cashless request not approved
In this fictional explanation, a cashless authorization decision concerns the requested payment route and does not by itself establish the final outcome of a reimbursement claim. A member should ask for the reason, confirm the correct claim route and keep bills and medical papers for the insurer's secure process. Possible matters for clarification include hospital participation, missing paperwork and the policy's eligibility terms; this FAQ cannot diagnose a particular denial. Reimbursement may still be reviewed under the policy, but it is never promised. No real hospital network or contact number is supplied.

## Renewal and lapse
In this fictional teaching example, renewal concerns keeping a policy active for another term under the insurer's conditions. A lapse means the policy is no longer active under its applicable terms; paying later does not automatically establish cover for a past expense. An illustrative next step is to check the expiry date, renewal terms and insurer confirmation before assuming continuous cover. This FAQ gives no grace period, late-payment rule, premium amount or reinstatement guarantee. It cannot confirm whether a specific real policy is active.

## Pre-existing conditions
In this fictional teaching example, pre-existing conditions are health conditions identified under a policy's own definition and disclosure terms before cover begins. They can have different eligibility or waiting-period rules from an initial illness waiting period. The sample's initial illness waiting period is 30 days, but this FAQ specifies no waiting period or coverage for pre-existing conditions. An illustrative next step is to check the actual definition, exclusions and disclosure requirements with the insurer. Do not share diagnoses, prescriptions or medical records with this demo assistant.

## Policy schedule
In this fictional explanation, a policy schedule is the summary of the specific cover issued, while the policy wording explains the terms and conditions. Useful items to check include insured members, coverage dates, premium, sum insured, and any stated deductible or co-payment. An illustrative example: a marketing description of cashless service cannot replace checking the issued schedule and eligibility clauses. This FAQ contains no issued policy and cannot verify a person's cover. Compare the actual schedule with the full wording and ask the insurer about unclear or inconsistent details.

## Nominee
In this fictional teaching example, a nominee is a person designated under the policy's applicable nomination rules for receiving a benefit when those rules apply. Naming someone as a nominee does not automatically make them an insured family member or give them treatment coverage. This FAQ specifies no benefit amount, nomination form, succession rule or legal entitlement. An illustrative next step is to check the nominee entry and relevant terms with the actual insurer. Do not enter a nominee's identity or personal details into this demo.

## Room rent and sub-limits
In this fictional teaching example, a sub-limit is a separate limit for a particular benefit or expense within the overall coverage conditions. A room-rent limit concerns the room expense under the policy's wording. An illustrative example: a policy's overall sum insured does not alone establish that a particular room category or treatment expense is fully payable. This FAQ gives no room category, expense amount, proportional deduction formula or treatment-specific sub-limit. Ask the actual insurer to explain the applicable clause before estimating out-of-pocket costs.

## Claim status and review
In this fictional demo, a pending claim means a review is ongoing; a request for documents asks for information and does not promise approval. To understand a real decision, ask the insurer for the written reason, the relevant policy clause and its official review or grievance process. Keep any claim reference and correspondence in the insurer's secure channel. This FAQ provides no claim tracking system, decision date, appeal deadline, escalation contact or regulatory rule. The assistant cannot access a person's claim or change its status.

## Choosing what to ask
This fictional demo explains premium, sum insured, waiting periods, deductibles, co-payment, family floater concepts, claim documents and the cashless versus reimbursement routes. Useful questions include asking for a plain-language definition, a supported example or a comparison of related terms. For a particular policy, check its issued schedule, wording, exclusions and insurer confirmation. This FAQ does not recommend an insurer, rank products, quote a price, check a real hospital network or guarantee coverage. It is a learning aid for testing conversation, not personalized buying or medical advice.
